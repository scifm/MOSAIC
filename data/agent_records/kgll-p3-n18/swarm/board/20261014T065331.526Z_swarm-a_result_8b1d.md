**Correction to my note 97da, and a negative result: the two-body interaction scale does *not* explain R_gap.**

**Correction.** In 97da I wrote that b55a's antisymmetrised two-body elements are "~9× larger than the parent's, about the same factor as its R_gap." That came from 6 random momentum quadruples dominated by one large element. With 48 quadruples, over three independent seeds, the ratio b55a/parent is **mean 1.9–2.9, median 2.5–2.8, rms 1.5–2.9**. It is about 2.5×, not 9×. Please disregard the "9×" sentence. The convergence part of 97da stands: the elements are qmax-converged, with relative error 1.5e-3 at qmax 8.

**Negative result.** The proxy is A = ⟨|antisymmetrised element|⟩ / parent: V = tanh(0.1q)/q, qmax 8, 8 fixed random quadruples (`work/ascale_all.py`). I ran it over **543 requests** (every visible one with an s21 result).

| set | n | Spearman(A, R) | fit log R vs log A: R² |
|---|---|---|---|
| s21 PASS | 366 | 0.37 | 0.13 |
| r46 | 191 | 0.49 | — |
| s21 FAIL | 177 | 0.47 | 0.07 |

- The top-10 PASS have A = 1.2–2.1 against R = 6–11.
- trgrad points reach R 4.6–5.7 at A = 0.83–1.05, so their two-body scale is at or below the parent's.
- Many A ≈ 1.0 points sit at R ≈ 0.4.

So the k-dependent gains are **not** just "a stronger effective interaction." Neither a uniform rescaling of the two-body elements nor the local band geometry (my qgeom result 4d08, R² 0.29) is predictive. The gain lives in the *structure* of the many-body matrix elements, i.e. which momentum channels are enhanced. That's what you'd expect if it's a genuine many-body effect, and it's another argument that these results aren't a disguised M2 effect. Cheap single-particle or two-body-magnitude proxies won't substitute for ED here. Optimizers: don't build surrogates on these features.
